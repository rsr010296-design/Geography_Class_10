01 · RESOURCES

What is a Resource?

📖 Definition: Everything available in our environment which can be used to satisfy our needs, provided it is technologically accessible, economically feasible and culturally acceptable can be termed as a Resource.

🔺 The Resource Triangle — Interdependent Relationship

N
Physical Environment (Nature)

Provides the raw material available in our surroundings.

T
Technology

Humans interact with nature through technology to transform material into resources.

I
Institutions

Created by human beings to accelerate economic development and regulate resource use.

💡 Key Idea: Resources are not free gifts of nature. They are a function of human activities. Human beings themselves are essential components of resources — they transform material into resources and use them.

02 · RESOURCE PLANNING & SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT

Development of Resources & Sustainable Development

⚠️ Problems Due to Indiscriminate Use

1
Depletion of Resources

Greedy exploitation by a few individuals for personal gain leads to rapid depletion.

2
Socio-Economic Inequality

Accumulation of resources in few hands divided society into haves and have-nots (rich and poor).

3
Global Ecological Crises

Global warming, ozone layer depletion, environmental pollution and land degradation.

🌿 Sustainable Development

Definition: Development should take place without damaging the environment, and development in the present should not compromise with the needs of future generations.

▶
Brundtland Commission Report, 1987

Introduced the concept of 'Sustainable Development'. Published as Our Common Future.

▶
Club of Rome, 1968

First advocated resource conservation in a systematic way at the international level.

▶
Schumacher — Small is Beautiful (1974)

Presented Gandhian philosophy on resource conservation at international level.

There is enough for everybody's need and not for anybody's greed.

— MAHATMA GANDHI · On Resource Conservation
🌐

Rio de Janeiro Earth Summit, 1992

More than 100 heads of states met in Brazil for the first International Earth Summit. Signed the Declaration on Global Climatic Change and Biological Diversity. Endorsed global Forest Principles and adopted Agenda 21.

📜

Agenda 21

Declaration signed at UNCED (United Nations Conference on Environment and Development), Rio de Janeiro, 1992. Aims to combat environmental damage, poverty and disease through global co-operation. Every local government must draw its own Local Agenda 21.

🗺️ Resource Planning in India

Why needed? India has enormous diversity in availability of resources. Some regions are resource-rich but economically backward (e.g. Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, MP — rich in minerals & coal). Some are resource-poor but economically developed.

I
Identification & Inventory

Surveying, mapping, qualitative and quantitative estimation and measurement of resources across all regions.

II
Planning Structure

Evolving a structure with appropriate technology, skill and institutional set-up for implementing resource development plans.

III
Matching with National Plans

Resource development plans must align with overall national development plans (since the First Five Year Plan after Independence).

RESOURCE RICH — Yet Economically Backward

  • Jharkhand — minerals, coal
  • Chhattisgarh — minerals
  • Madhya Pradesh — forests, minerals
  • Arunachal Pradesh — water resources

SPECIAL RESOURCE SITUATIONS

  • Rajasthan — solar & wind rich, water scarce
  • Ladakh — rich cultural heritage, lacks water & minerals
  • Punjab & Haryana — net sown area >80%
  • NE states — net sown area <10%
03 · LAND RESOURCES

Land Resources & Land Use

🌍 Land is a natural resource of utmost importance. It supports natural vegetation, wildlife, human life, economic activities, transport and communication systems. India's total geographical area = 3.28 million sq km. Land use data available for 93% of total area.

📊 Land Utilisation Categories NCERT Fig. 1.4

1
Forests

Forest area is far lower than the desired 33% as outlined in the National Forest Policy (1952) for ecological balance.

2
Land Not Available for Cultivation

(a) Barren & waste land (rocky, arid, desert areas)
(b) Land put to non-agricultural uses — buildings, roads, railways, factories

3
Other Uncultivated Land (excl. fallow)

(a) Permanent pastures & grazing land
(b) Land under miscellaneous tree crops & groves
(c) Culturable waste land (uncultivated >5 agricultural years)

4
Fallow Lands

(a) Current fallow — uncultivated ≤1 agricultural year
(b) Other than current fallow — uncultivated 1–5 years

5
Net Sown Area (NSA)

Physical extent of land on which crops are sown and harvested. NSA + area sown more than once = Gross Cropped Area.

NSA India ≈ 45.64% | Punjab & Haryana >80% | NE states <10%

Land Degradation & Conservation Measures

⚠️ 95% of our basic needs for food, shelter and clothing are obtained from land. Continuous use without conservation measures causes land degradation with serious socio-economic and environmental repercussions.

State / RegionCause of Land DegradationConservation Measure
Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh, MP, OdishaDeforestation due to mining; deep scars after excavationAfforestation; proper management of mining activities

04 · SOIL AS A RESOURCE

Types of Soil in India

🌱 Soil is the most important natural resource. It is the medium of plant growth and supports living organisms. Soil formation takes millions of years. Factors: relief, parent rock, climate, vegetation, and time. Soil consists of organic (humus) and inorganic materials. NCERT Fig. 1.5 — Soil Profile

🖱️ Hover or tap a soil card to see detailed properties

🏔️

Alluvial Soil

tap to explore ▼

ALLUVIAL SOIL

  • Most widespread & important soil
  • Entire Northern Plains; also Rajasthan & Gujarat corridor; eastern coastal deltas (Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri)
  • Deposited by Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra
  • Bangar — old alluvial (higher kanker nodules)
  • Khadar — new alluvial (more fertile, finer)
  • Rich in potash, phosphoric acid, lime
  • Ideal for sugarcane, paddy, wheat, cereals & pulses
  • Intensively cultivated, densely populated regions
⚫

Black Soil

tap to explore ▼

BLACK SOIL (REGUR)

  • Also known as Regur or Black Cotton Soil
  • Typical of Deccan Trap (Basalt) region
  • Covers Maharashtra, Saurashtra, Malwa, MP, Chhattisgarh
  • Extends SE along Godavari & Krishna valleys
  • Made of extremely fine clayey material
  • Holds moisture well; rich in Ca, Mg, potash, lime
  • Ideal for cotton cultivation
  • Sticky when wet; develops deep cracks in hot weather (helps aeration)
  • Generally poor in phosphoric content
🔴

Red & Yellow Soil

tap to explore ▼

RED & YELLOW SOIL

  • Develops on crystalline igneous rocks
  • Found in eastern & southern parts of Deccan plateau (low rainfall areas)
  • Also in Odisha, Chhattisgarh, southern middle Ganga plain, Western Ghats piedmont
  • Red colour due to diffusion of iron in crystalline & metamorphic rocks
  • Looks yellow when in hydrated form
🧱

Laterite Soil

tap to explore ▼

LATERITE SOIL

  • From Latin 'later' = brick
  • Develops under tropical & subtropical climate with alternate wet & dry seasons
  • Result of intense leaching due to heavy rain
  • Mostly deep to very deep; acidic (pH < 6.0)
  • Generally deficient in plant nutrients
  • Found in southern states, Western Ghats (Maharashtra), Odisha, parts of West Bengal, NE regions
  • After conservation: useful for tea & coffee (Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu)
  • Red laterite (Tamil Nadu, AP, Kerala): suitable for cashew nut
🏜️

Arid Soil

tap to explore ▼

ARID SOIL

  • Red to brown in colour
  • Generally sandy in texture and saline in nature
  • In some areas, salt content very high — common salt obtained by evaporating water
  • High temperature → faster evaporation → lacks humus & moisture
  • Lower horizons occupied by Kankar (increasing calcium downwards)
  • Kankar layer restricts infiltration of water
  • After proper irrigation, cultivable — e.g. western Rajasthan
🌲

Forest Soil

tap to explore ▼

FOREST SOIL

  • Found in hilly & mountainous areas with sufficient rain forests
  • Texture varies with mountain environment
  • Loamy & silty on valley sides; coarse-grained on upper slopes
  • In snow-covered Himalayan areas: acidic, low humus (denudation)
  • Soils on river terraces & alluvial fans in lower valleys are fertile

05 · SOIL EROSION & CONSERVATION

Soil Erosion & Conservation

🌊 Soil Erosion: The denudation of the soil cover and subsequent washing down is called soil erosion. Soil formation and erosion are ongoing processes; human activities disturb their natural balance.

🌊
Gully Erosion

Running water cuts through clayey soils, making deep channels called gullies. Land becomes unfit for cultivation — called bad land. In the Chambal basin, such lands are called ravines.

Remedy: Afforestation, Check dams
💧
Sheet Erosion

Water flows as a sheet over large areas down a slope, washing away the top soil uniformly. Results in loss of the most fertile top layer.

Remedy: Contour ploughing, Terrace farming
💨
Wind Erosion

Wind blows loose soil off flat or sloping land. Common in arid and semi-arid regions like Rajasthan.

Remedy: Shelter belts, Stabilisation of sand dunes with thorny bushes

🌱 Soil Conservation Methods

🌾

Contour Ploughing

Ploughing along contour lines decelerates the flow of water down slopes, reducing soil erosion.

🏔️

Terrace Cultivation

Steps cut out on slopes making terraces restricts erosion. Well developed in Western & Central Himalayas.

🌿

Strip Cropping

Large fields divided into strips. Strips of grass left to grow between crops to break up the force of wind.

🌳

Shelter Belts

Rows of trees planted to create shelter against wind. Have contributed significantly to stabilisation of sand dunes in western India.

📝 Short Answer Questions (30 words) NCERT Textbook

  1. Name three states having black soil and the crop which is mainly grown in it.
    Three states with black soil: Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, and Chhattisgarh (also: Saurashtra, Malwa region). The crop mainly grown in black soil is cotton, which is why it is also called 'Black Cotton Soil' or 'Regur soil'. It is also suitable for other crops like wheat, jowar, linseed, and millets.
  2. What type of soil is found in the river deltas of the eastern coast? Give three main features of this type of soil.
    Alluvial soil is found in the river deltas of the eastern coast (e.g. deltas of Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri rivers). Three main features: (i) Very fertile — contains adequate proportions of potash, phosphoric acid and lime. (ii) Ideal for growing sugarcane, paddy, wheat, cereal and pulse crops. (iii) Found in various textures ranging from sandy loam to clay, and classified as Bangar (old) and Khadar (new) based on age.
  3. What steps can be taken to control soil erosion in the hilly areas?
    Steps to control soil erosion in hilly areas: (i) Contour ploughing — ploughing along contour lines decelerates the flow of water down the slopes. (ii) Terrace cultivation — steps cut out on slopes making terraces restrict erosion (practised in western and central Himalayas). (iii) Strip cropping — strips of grass left between crops break up the force of wind. (iv) Afforestation — planting trees reduces impact of rainfall on soil. (v) Shelter belts — rows of trees planted to protect soil from wind erosion.

📖 Long Answer Questions (120 words) NCERT Textbook

  1. Explain land use pattern in India and why has the land under forest not increased much since 1960–61?
    Land use in India is determined by physical factors (topography, climate, soil types) and human factors (population density, technological capability, culture). Land is used for forests, land not available for cultivation (barren land, non-agricultural uses), other uncultivated land (permanent pastures, miscellaneous tree crops, culturable wasteland), fallow lands, and net sown area (45.64% as of 2019–20). Forest cover remains far below the desired 33% as per the National Forest Policy (1952). Reasons: (i) Rapid growth of human population has put pressure on land for agriculture, housing and infrastructure. (ii) Rampant deforestation for cultivation, mining, and industry. (iii) Encroachments on forest land for agriculture. (iv) Diversion of forest land for development projects. The result is that forest area has not increased significantly from 18–22% range since 1960–61.
  2. How have technical and economic development led to more consumption of resources?
    Technical and economic development has accelerated resource consumption in multiple ways: (i) Industrialisation: The growth of industries demands large quantities of minerals, energy resources (coal, petroleum) and water, depleting non-renewable resources rapidly. (ii) Agricultural modernisation: Green Revolution introduced chemical fertilisers, pesticides, and heavy irrigation, leading to soil degradation, waterlogging and over-extraction of groundwater. (iii) Urbanisation: Expansion of cities converts agricultural and forest land to non-agricultural uses, roads, buildings and infrastructure. (iv) Technology-driven lifestyle: Modern technology has created a culture of mass production and mass consumption (as Gandhiji warned), increasing per capita resource use. (v) Colonialism: Historically, technologically advanced colonising nations exploited natural resources of other countries to fuel their own development, establishing a pattern of extractive resource use. This demonstrates that resources alone are insufficient for development — they must be accompanied by appropriate institutional changes and sustainable practices.

📋 Project 1 — Consumption & Conservation NCERT Textbook

Make a project showing consumption and conservation of resources in your locality.

1
Identify key resources

List all major resources used in your locality — water, land, forest, minerals, energy (electricity, fuel). Note how each is consumed daily.

2
Analyse consumption patterns

Which resources are being over-used? Which are being wasted? How has consumption changed over the past decade?

3
Document conservation efforts

What conservation steps are already being taken — rainwater harvesting, solar panels, tree plantations, waste segregation?

4
Prepare your project

Present your findings as a report/poster with maps, charts, photographs or drawings. Suggest 3 new conservation measures for your locality.

💬 Activity 2 — Class Discussion: School Resources NCERT Textbook

Have a discussion in the class — how to conserve various resources used in your school.

Consider these resources used in school and suggest conservation measures:

💧
Water

Taps left running, leaks, overuse in toilets and gardens. Conservation: fix leaks, turn off taps, use buckets instead of hoses.

⚡
Electricity

Fans and lights left on in empty rooms. Conservation: motion sensors, switching off when leaving, use natural light.

📄
Paper

Single-use printing, torn notebooks. Conservation: double-sided printing, use both sides of notebooks, recycle.

🌳
Green Spaces

School garden and trees provide shade, reduce heat island effect. Conservation: plant more trees, maintain garden.

💡 Discussion Points:

• Which resources are most wasted in your school?

• What simple habits would help conserve them?

• Can your school become a model of sustainable resource use?

• Who is responsible for conservation — students, teachers or management?

🛢️ Activity 3 — Imagine: If Oil Runs Out NCERT Textbook

🤔 Imagine if oil supplies get exhausted — how will this affect our life style?

🚗

Transport

Petrol and diesel vehicles cannot run. Roads and highways become useless. Airlines shut down. People must shift to electric vehicles, cycles, bullock carts, or walking.

🏭

Industries

Thousands of industries dependent on petroleum-based chemicals, plastics and fertilisers will collapse. Food production will drop sharply as tractors stop working.

🏠

Daily Life

Cooking gas (LPG) disappears. Electricity (from thermal plants) will fail in many areas. Heating and cooling systems stop. People must return to firewood, solar, wind.

🌱

Opportunity

The crisis forces a shift to renewable energy — solar, wind, hydro. Local, sustainable lifestyles revive. Communities become more self-reliant. A true green economy begins.

📌 Key lesson: This scenario explains why sustainable development and the shift to renewable energy is not just an option — it is a necessity. Gandhiji said: "There is enough for everybody's need but not for any body's greed."
