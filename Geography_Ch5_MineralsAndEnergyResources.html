01 · MINERALS — DEFINITION & OCCURRENCE

What is a Mineral?

🔬 Definition: Geologists define a mineral as a "homogenous, naturally occurring substance with a definable internal structure." Minerals are found in varied forms in nature, ranging from the hardest diamond to the softest talc. Over 2000 minerals have been identified, but only a few are abundantly found in most rocks.

🪨 Mode of Occurrence of Minerals

i
Veins & Lodes (Igneous & Metamorphic Rocks)

Minerals occur in cracks, crevices, faults or joints. Smaller = veins; larger = lodes. Formed when minerals in liquid/molten & gaseous forms are forced upward. Major metallic minerals: tin, copper, zinc, lead.

ii
Beds / Layers (Sedimentary Rocks)

Formed by deposition, accumulation in horizontal strata. Coal, iron ore (heat & pressure); gypsum, potash salt, sodium salt (evaporation in arid regions).

iii
Residual Deposits (Weathering)

Decomposition of surface rocks, removal of soluble constituents leaves residual mass. Bauxite is formed this way.

iv
Placer Deposits (Alluvial)

Minerals in sands of valley floors and base of hills. Contain minerals not corroded by water: gold, silver, tin, platinum.

v
Ocean Waters

Vast quantities but too diffused for most. Exception: common salt, magnesium, bromine. Ocean beds rich in manganese nodules.

🗺️ Distribution of Minerals in India:

• Peninsular rocks — most coal, metallic minerals, mica, non-metallic minerals
• Gujarat & Assam — most petroleum deposits (sedimentary rocks, western & eastern flanks)
• Rajasthan — many non-ferrous minerals
• North Indian alluvial plains — almost devoid of economic minerals

🐀 Rat-Hole Mining (Meghalaya): In tribal areas of NE India, minerals are owned by individuals/communities. Coal mining in Jowai and Cherapunjee is done by families in long narrow tunnels — 'Rat hole' mining. The National Green Tribunal has declared such activities illegal and ordered them stopped.

📌 Conservation: Total workable mineral deposits = only 1% of Earth's crust. Mineral resources are finite and non-renewable. Geological processes of mineral formation are infinitely slow compared to current rates of consumption.

02 · FERROUS MINERALS

Ferrous Minerals

⚙️ Ferrous minerals account for about three-fourths of the total value of production of metallic minerals. They provide a strong base for metallurgical industries. India exports substantial quantities after meeting internal demands.

🖤 Coal — Most Abundant Fossil Fuel in India

Coal occurs in two main geological age groups: Gondwana (~200 million years old) and Tertiary (~55 million years old).

⚠️ Coal is a bulky material — loses weight on use (reduced to ash). Hence, heavy industries and thermal power stations are located near coalfields.

🛢️ Petroleum

Petroleum provides fuel for heat and lighting, lubricants for machinery and raw materials for manufacturing industries. Petroleum refineries act as a "nodal industry" for synthetic textile, fertiliser and chemical industries.

O
Occurrence

Associated with anticlines and fault traps in tertiary rock formations. Oil trapped in the crest of upfold (anticline/dome). Oil bearing layer = porous limestone or sandstone. Gas (lighter) usually occurs above the oil.

1
Mumbai High

Major offshore petroleum field — largest producer. Also: Bassien.

2
Gujarat

Ankleshwar — most important field of Gujarat. Also Kalol, Vadodara, Jhabua fields.

3
Assam

Oldest oil producing state. Digboi, Naharkatiya, Moran-Hugrijan — important oil fields.

💨

Natural Gas

Found with petroleum; released when crude oil is brought to surface. Used as fuel in power sector, industrial heating, raw material in chemical/petrochemical/fertiliser industries, transport fuel (CNG) and cooking fuel (PNG).

Major reserves: Mumbai High (west coast), Cambay basin, Krishna-Godavari basin (east coast — new finds).

HVJ Pipeline: 1,700 km Hazira–Vijaipur–Jagdishpur — GAIL's first cross-country gas pipeline. Network expanded to 18,500 km; expected 34,000 km as Gas Grid.

⚡

Electricity

Per-capita electricity consumption is considered an index of development. Generated in two ways:

🌊 Hydro Electricity — Fast flowing water drives hydro turbines. Renewable resource. Projects: Bhakra Nangal, Damodar Valley Corporation, Kopili Hydel Project.

🔥 Thermal Electricity — Coal, petroleum, natural gas used to drive turbines. Uses non-renewable fossil fuels.

04 · NON-CONVENTIONAL ENERGY SOURCES

Non-Conventional Energy Sources

🌿 Growing dependence on fossil fuels + rising prices + environmental problems = urgent need for renewable energy sources. India is blessed with abundance of sunlight, water, wind and biomass — it has the largest programmes for developing these resources.

☀️

Solar Energy

India is a tropical country with enormous potential. Photovoltaic technology converts sunlight directly into electricity. Fast becoming popular in rural and remote areas. Reduces dependence on firewood and dung cakes → environmental conservation and adequate manure for agriculture.

💨

Wind Power

Largest wind farm cluster: Tamil Nadu — Nagarcoil to Madurai. Also: Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Gujarat, Kerala, Maharashtra, Lakshadweep. Nagarcoil and Jaisalmer are well known for effective wind energy use.

🌿

Biogas

Shrubs, farm waste, animal and human waste produce biogas for domestic consumption. Higher thermal efficiency than kerosene, dung cake, charcoal. Plants using cattle dung = 'Gobar gas plants'. Twin benefit: energy + improved manure quality. Biogas is most efficient use of cattle dung.

🌊

Tidal Energy

Floodgate dams built across inlets. High tide water trapped → flows back through turbine as tide falls. Ideal locations in India: Gulf of Khambhat, Gulf of Kuchchh (Gujarat, west coast), Gangetic delta — Sunderban (West Bengal).

🌋

Geothermal Energy

Heat from Earth's interior. High geothermal gradient → hot groundwater → steam → drives turbines. Several hundred hot springs in India. Two experimental projects: Parvati Valley, Manikaran (Himachal Pradesh) and Puga Valley, Ladakh.

⚛️

Nuclear / Atomic Energy

Obtained by altering atomic structure → heat → electricity. Uranium and Thorium used. Found in Jharkhand and Aravalli ranges (Rajasthan). Monazite sands of Kerala also rich in Thorium. 6 nuclear power stations in India.

💡 Conservation of Energy: India is one of the least energy efficient countries in the world. Steps citizens can take: use public transport, switch off electricity when not in use, use power-saving devices, use non-conventional sources. "Energy saved is energy produced."

📝 Short Answer Questions NCERT

  1. Distinguish between: (a) Ferrous and Non-ferrous minerals; (b) Conventional and Non-conventional sources of energy.
    (a) Ferrous vs Non-Ferrous:
    Ferrous minerals contain iron (e.g. iron ore, manganese, nickel, cobalt). They account for ~three-fourths of total metallic mineral production value and provide a base for metallurgical industries.
    Non-ferrous minerals do not contain iron (e.g. copper, bauxite, lead, zinc, gold). India's reserves of non-ferrous minerals are not very satisfactory but they are vital for metallurgical, engineering and electrical industries.

    (b) Conventional vs Non-Conventional Energy:
    Conventional sources include firewood, cattle dung cake, coal, petroleum, natural gas and electricity (hydel and thermal). These are traditional energy sources, many of which are non-renewable and cause environmental pollution.
    Non-conventional sources include solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, biogas and atomic energy. These are renewable, environmentally friendly and India is rapidly developing these to reduce dependence on fossil fuels.
  2. What is a mineral?
    Geologists define a mineral as a "homogenous, naturally occurring substance with a definable internal structure." Minerals are found in varied forms in nature, ranging from the hardest diamond to the softest talc. They are formed from specific combinations of elements under particular physical and chemical conditions, which result in a wide range of colours, hardness, crystal forms, lustre and density. Over 2000 minerals have been identified, but only a few are abundantly found in most rocks.
  3. How are minerals formed in igneous and metamorphic rocks?
    In igneous and metamorphic rocks, minerals occur in cracks, crevices, faults or joints. They are formed when minerals in liquid/molten and gaseous forms are forced upward through cavities towards the Earth's surface. As they rise, they cool and solidify. Smaller occurrences are called veins and larger ones are called lodes. Major metallic minerals obtained from veins and lodes include tin, copper, zinc and lead.
  4. Why do we need to conserve mineral resources?
    We need to conserve mineral resources because: (i) The total volume of workable mineral deposits is an insignificant fraction — only 1% of the Earth's crust; (ii) Mineral resources are finite and non-renewable — once used, they cannot be replaced; (iii) We are rapidly consuming minerals that required millions of years to be created and concentrated; (iv) The geological processes of mineral formation are so slow that the rates of replenishment are infinitely small compared to present consumption rates; (v) Continued extraction leads to increasing costs as extraction occurs from greater depths with decreasing quality. Conservation strategies include recycling of metals, using scrap metals, using substitutes and employing improved technologies to use low-grade ores.

📖 Long Answer Questions NCERT

  1. Describe the distribution of coal in India.
    Coal is the most abundantly available fossil fuel in India. It provides a substantial part of the nation's energy needs for power generation, industry and domestic use. Coal in India occurs in rock series of two main geological ages:
    1. Gondwana Coal (~200 million years old): These are metallurgical coals and are located in the Damodar Valley (West Bengal–Jharkhand) — Jharia, Raniganj, Bokaro are important coalfields. The Godavari, Mahanadi, Son and Wardha valleys also contain coal deposits. This is the major type of coal in India.
    2. Tertiary Coal (~55 million years old): Found in the north-eastern states of Meghalaya, Assam, Arunachal Pradesh and Nagaland.
    Types of coal: Peat (lowest grade) → Lignite (brown, soft; Neyveli, Tamil Nadu) → Bituminous (most commercially used) → Anthracite (highest quality). Since coal is a bulky material that loses weight on use (reduced to ash), heavy industries and thermal power stations are strategically located near coalfields.
  2. Why do you think that solar energy has a bright future in India?
    Solar energy has a bright future in India for the following reasons: (i) Tropical location — India receives abundant, intense sunlight throughout the year due to its tropical position, making it ideal for solar power generation; (ii) Photovoltaic technology — advances in technology allow direct conversion of sunlight into electricity efficiently and at reducing costs; (iii) Rural electrification — solar energy is fast becoming popular in rural and remote areas where grid electricity is unavailable or unreliable; (iv) Environmental benefit — solar energy is clean and renewable, reducing dependence on firewood and dung cakes which cause deforestation and deplete manure; (v) Large solar programmes — India has established some of the world's largest solar power plants; (vi) Energy security — reducing dependence on imported petroleum and coal improves national energy security; (vii) Low maintenance — solar panels require minimal maintenance and have long operational life; (viii) Government support — India has the largest programmes for development of renewable energy resources.
