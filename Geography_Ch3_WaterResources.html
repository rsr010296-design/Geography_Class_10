01 · WATER SCARCITY

Water Scarcity & the Need for Conservation

🔄 Water is a Renewable Resource — All water moves within the hydrological cycle, ensuring continuous renewal. Freshwater is obtained from surface run-off and groundwater, continually recharged through the hydrological cycle.

⚠️ The Paradox: Three-fourths of Earth is covered with water — yet by 2025, nearly 2 billion people will live in absolute water scarcity. Water scarcity in most cases is caused by over-exploitation, excessive use and unequal access among different social groups.

🏙️ An area with ample water can still face scarcity if: (i) water is polluted by domestic/industrial wastes, chemicals, pesticides; (ii) population is large with unequal access; (iii) water is over-exploited for irrigated agriculture.

02 · MULTI-PURPOSE RIVER PROJECTS

Multi-purpose River Projects & Dams

Dams are the temples of modern India; they would integrate development of agriculture and the village economy with rapid industrialisation and growth of the urban economy.

— JAWAHARLAL NEHRU · On Multi-purpose River Projects

🏛️ What is a Dam? A barrier across flowing water that obstructs, directs or retards the flow, often creating a reservoir, lake or impoundment. "Dam" refers to the reservoir rather than the structure. Most dams have a spillway or weir over which water flows.

⚙️ Uses of Multi-purpose Projects

1
Irrigation

Traditionally built to impound river and rainwater for irrigating agricultural fields. Irrigated agriculture is the largest consumer of water.

2
Electricity Generation

Hydroelectric (hydel) power — industries require power to run; much of this energy comes from hydroelectric power plants built as part of dams.

3
Flood Control

Regulating water flow to protect downstream areas. E.g. Hirakud project on Mahanadi integrates conservation of water with flood control.

4
Domestic & Industrial Water Supply

Water supply for urban and industrial uses, inland navigation, recreation and fish breeding.

03 · PROBLEMS WITH MULTI-PURPOSE PROJECTS

Advantages & Disadvantages of Dams

✅ ADVANTAGES

  • Irrigation to drought-prone and arid areas
  • Generation of hydroelectric (hydel) power
  • Domestic and industrial water supply
  • Flood control and regulation of water flow
  • Inland navigation
  • Recreation and fish breeding
  • Integrates agriculture with industrialisation

❌ DISADVANTAGES & PROBLEMS

  • Poor sediment flow; excessive sedimentation at reservoir bottom → rockier stream beds
  • Fragments rivers — aquatic fauna cannot migrate or spawn
  • Reservoirs submerge existing vegetation and soil → decomposition
  • Dams meant to control floods ironically triggered floods due to sedimentation
  • Soil erosion; flood plains deprived of silt (natural fertiliser) → land degradation
  • Induced earthquakes, water-borne diseases, pollution
  • Large-scale displacement of people (loss of livelihood)
  • Irrigation-driven shift to water-intensive crops → salinisation of soil
  • Inter-state water disputes (e.g. Krishna-Godavari dispute)

⚠️ Inter-State Water Disputes: The Krishna-Godavari dispute arose due to objections raised by Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh over the diversion of more water at Koyna by Maharashtra government for a multipurpose project — which would reduce downstream flow adversely affecting agriculture and industry.

04 · RAINWATER HARVESTING

Rainwater Harvesting in India

🌧️ Water harvesting was seen as a viable alternative to multi-purpose projects — both socio-economically and environmentally. India had an extraordinary ancient tradition of water-harvesting with in-depth knowledge of rainfall regimes, soil types and local ecological conditions.

Guls / Kuls
Western Himalayas

Diversion channels built in hill and mountainous regions to divert stream and spring water to agricultural fields. Used extensively in states like Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand.

05 · MODERN ADAPTATIONS & INTERESTING FACTS

State-wise Water Conservation Facts

⚖️

Tamil Nadu — Legal Mandate

First state in India to make rooftop rainwater harvesting structure compulsory for all houses across the state. Legal provisions to punish defaulters.

🌧️

Shillong, Meghalaya — Paradox

Most common rooftop harvesting practice. Despite Cherapunjee and Mawsynram (55 km away) receiving the highest rainfall in the world, Shillong faces acute water shortage. 15–25% of household water needs met through rooftop harvesting.

🏜️

Western Rajasthan — Decline

Traditional tanka practice is declining as the perennial Indira Gandhi Canal now provides plenty of water. Though some houses still maintain tankas as they prefer the taste of stored rainwater.

🔄

Rooftop System — How it Works

✏️ Identify & Correct False Statements NCERT Q.1(iii)

❌ (a) "Multiplying urban centres with large and dense populations and urban lifestyles have helped in proper utilisation of water resources."

✅ Correction: Multiplying urban centres with large and dense populations and urban lifestyles have NOT helped — instead, they have added to water and energy requirements and further aggravated the problem by over-exploiting groundwater.

❌ (b) "Regulating and damming of rivers does not affect the river's natural flow and its sediment flow."

✅ Correction: Regulating and damming of rivers does affect their natural flow — it causes poor sediment flow and excessive sedimentation at the bottom of the reservoir, resulting in rockier stream beds and poorer habitats for aquatic life.

❌ (c) "Today in Rajasthan, the practice of rooftop rainwater harvesting has gained popularity despite high water availability due to the Indira Gandhi Canal."

✅ Correction: Today in western Rajasthan, the practice of rooftop rainwater harvesting is on the decline as plenty of water is available due to the perennial Indira Gandhi Canal. (Though some houses still maintain tankas as they prefer the taste of rainwater.)

📝 Short Answer Questions (30 words) NCERT

  1. Explain how water becomes a renewable resource.
    Water becomes a renewable resource through the hydrological cycle. All water on Earth moves continuously within this cycle — water from oceans, rivers and lakes evaporates, rises to form clouds, condenses and falls back as precipitation (rain, snow). This precipitation becomes surface run-off and groundwater, which are continually being renewed and recharged. This endless cycling ensures that water, though it can be locally scarce, is globally a renewable resource.
  2. What is water scarcity and what are its main causes?
    Water scarcity is the shortage of freshwater to meet the demands of water usage — for drinking, agriculture, industry and daily use. Main causes: (i) Over-exploitation and excessive use of water resources, particularly by irrigated agriculture (the largest consumer); (ii) Unequal access among different social groups; (iii) Large and growing population increasing demand for food and water; (iv) Industrialisation and urbanisation exerting pressure on freshwater; (v) Water pollution — domestic and industrial wastes, chemicals and pesticides making water hazardous; (vi) Falling groundwater levels due to over-pumping by tube-wells and wells.
  3. Compare the advantages and disadvantages of multi-purpose river projects.
    Advantages: (i) Irrigation to drought-prone and arid areas; (ii) Generation of hydroelectric power; (iii) Flood control; (iv) Water supply for domestic and industrial use; (v) Inland navigation, recreation, fish breeding. Disadvantages: (i) Poor sediment flow → rockier stream beds, poorer aquatic habitats; (ii) Fragmentation of rivers blocking migration of aquatic fauna; (iii) Submergence of existing vegetation causing decomposition; (iv) Triggered floods due to sedimentation; (v) Deprivation of flood plains from silt (natural fertiliser) causing land degradation; (vi) Induced earthquakes, water-borne diseases; (vii) Large-scale displacement of communities; (viii) Shift to water-intensive crops causing salinisation; (ix) Inter-state water disputes.

📖 Long Answer Questions (120 words) NCERT

  1. Discuss how rainwater harvesting in semi-arid regions of Rajasthan is carried out.
    Rainwater harvesting in the semi-arid and arid regions of Rajasthan — particularly in Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer — has been a centuries-old practice. Almost all houses traditionally had underground tanks called tankas for storing drinking water. These tankas were built inside the main house or courtyard and connected to the sloping roofs through pipes. Rainwater falling on rooftops flowed down the pipes into the underground tankas. The first spell of rain was usually not collected as it served to clean the roofs and pipes. Subsequent showers were then stored. Tankas could be very large — one in Phalodi was 6.1 metres deep, 4.27 metres long and 2.44 metres wide. The stored rainwater, called palar pani, was considered the purest form of natural water. It could be stored till the next rainfall, making it a reliable source particularly in summer. In arid regions, agricultural fields were also converted into rain-fed storage structures — khadins in Jaisalmer and johads in other parts of Rajasthan — where water was allowed to stand and moisten the soil for cultivation.
  2. Describe how modern adaptations of traditional rainwater harvesting methods are being carried out to conserve and store water.
    Traditional rainwater harvesting methods are being modernised and adapted across India: (i) Gendathur, Karnataka — In this remote village in Mysuru, 200 households installed rooftop rainwater harvesting systems. With 1,000 mm annual precipitation and 80% collection efficiency, each house collects ~50,000 litres per year, giving a total of 1,00,000 litres annually. (ii) Tamil Nadu — First state to make rooftop rainwater harvesting compulsory for all houses with legal provisions to punish defaulters. (iii) Shillong, Meghalaya — Nearly every household has a rooftop harvesting structure, meeting 15–25% of total water needs, despite the city facing acute shortage even though Cherapunjee nearby receives the world's highest rainfall. (iv) Modern Rooftop System — Rainwater is collected via PVC pipes, filtered through sand and bricks, stored in a sump for immediate use; excess water recharges the well, which in turn recharges groundwater (NCERT Fig. 3.3). (v) Bamboo Drip Irrigation, Meghalaya — A 200-year-old technique using bamboo pipes to transport stream water by gravity across hundreds of metres, delivering 20–80 drops per minute at plant roots — zero energy cost, zero pollution.
