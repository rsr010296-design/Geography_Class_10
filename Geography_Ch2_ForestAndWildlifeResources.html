01 · FLORA, FAUNA & BIODIVERSITY

Biodiversity in India

🌍 Biodiversity / Biological Diversity — Immensely rich in wildlife and cultivated species, diverse in form and function but closely integrated in a system through multiple networks of interdependencies. India is one of the world's richest countries in terms of its vast array of biological diversity.

🌿

Why Forests Matter

Forests play a key role in the ecological system — they are the primary producers on which all other living beings depend. Plants, animals and micro-organisms re-create the quality of air, water and soil essential for survival.

⚠️

Under Great Stress

Diverse flora and fauna are so well integrated in our daily lives that we take them for granted. But lately they are under great stress mainly due to insensitivity to our environment.

🧬

Why Conserve?

Conservation preserves ecological diversity and life support systems — water, air and soil. It also preserves the genetic diversity of plants and animals for better growth of species and breeding.

🐟

Dependent Livelihoods

Agriculture is still dependent on traditional crop varieties. Fisheries are heavily dependent on the maintenance of aquatic biodiversity. Millions live on the fringes of forests and depend on them.

The tree is a peculiar organism of unlimited kindness and benevolence and makes no demand for its sustenance, and extends generously the products of its life activity. It affords protection to all beings, offering shade even to the axemen who destroy it.

— GAUTAMA BUDDHA (487 B.C.)
02 · CONSERVATION & LEGAL PROTECTION

Conservation of Forests & Wildlife

📜 Key Legislation & Milestones

1960s–70s
Conservationists demanded a national wildlife protection programme amid rapid decline in wildlife population.
1972
Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act implemented. All-India list of protected species published. Banned hunting, gave legal protection to habitats, restricted trade in wildlife.
1973
Project Tiger launched. Tiger population had dwindled to just 1,827 from an estimated 55,000 at the turn of the century.
1980 & 1986
Notification under Wildlife Act added several hundred butterflies, moths, beetles and one dragonfly to the protected species list.
1988
Joint Forest Management (JFM) — Odisha passed the first resolution. Programme involves local communities in management of degraded forest land.
1991
For the first time, plants were added to the protected species list, starting with six species.

🐯 Project Tiger — In Detail

Launched: 1973 — One of the most well-publicised wildlife campaigns in the world. Tiger conservation is viewed not only as saving an endangered species but also as preserving biotypes of sizeable magnitude.

▼
Population Collapse

Tiger population dwindled from ~55,000 (early 1900s) to just 1,827 in 1973.

55,000 → 1,827 in ~70 years
!
Major Threats

Poaching for trade; shrinking habitat; depletion of prey base species; growing human population; trade of tiger skins; use of bones in traditional medicines (especially Asian countries).

⚑
India & Nepal — Prime Targets

Since India and Nepal provide habitat to about two-thirds of the surviving tiger population in the world, they became prime targets for poaching and illegal trading.

🏕️ Key Tiger Reserves: Corbett (Uttarakhand), Sunderbans (West Bengal), Bandhavgarh (MP), Sariska (Rajasthan), Manas (Assam), Periyar (Kerala)

03 · TYPES & DISTRIBUTION

Types of Forest Resources

🏛️ In India, much of forest and wildlife resources are either owned or managed by the government through the Forest Department or other government departments. Classified into three main categories.

I
Reserved Forests 🔒

More than half of the total forest land. Regarded as most valuable for conservation. Madhya Pradesh has the largest area under permanent forests (75% of its total forest area). J&K, AP, Uttarakhand, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal, Maharashtra have large percentages.

II
Protected Forests 🛡️

Almost one-third of total forest area. Declared by the Forest Department. Protected from any further depletion. Bihar, Haryana, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, Odisha and Rajasthan have a bulk of forests as protected forests.

III
Unclassed Forests 👥

Other forests and wastelands belonging to both government and private individuals and communities. All north-eastern states and parts of Gujarat have very high percentage of unclassed forests managed by local communities.

📌 Reserved + Protected Forests are together called Permanent Forest Estates — maintained for producing timber, other forest produce, and for protective reasons.

04 · FLORA & FAUNA — SPECIES STATUS

Species Status Categories

🔬 Flora and fauna are classified into different categories based on their survival status. Conservation projects now focus on biodiversity as a whole rather than on a few components. Even insects are finding a place in conservation planning.

🖱️ Hover or tap a card to learn more

💀

Extinct Species

tap to explore ▼

EXTINCT

  • Species not found in their habitat after searches
  • May be extinct from a local area, region, country or the entire earth
  • Examples: Asiatic cheetah (extinct in India), Pink-headed duck
  • Once gone, cannot be recovered
🚨

Endangered Species

tap to explore ▼

ENDANGERED

  • Species in danger of extinction
  • Survival is difficult if negative factors continue to operate
  • Examples: One-horned rhinoceros, Kashmir stag (hangul), Lion-tailed macaque, Sangai (brow-antlered deer), Wild Asian buffalo
  • Covered under Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972
⚠️

Vulnerable Species

tap to explore ▼

VULNERABLE

  • Species likely to move into endangered category if adverse conditions continue
  • Population has been reduced to a critically low level
  • Examples: Blue sheep, Asiatic elephant, Gangetic dolphin
  • Require monitoring and protective measures
📍

Endemic Species

tap to explore ▼

ENDEMIC

  • Species found only in some particular areas, usually isolated by natural or geographical barriers
  • Examples: Andaman teal, Nicobar pigeon, Andaman wild pig, Mithun (Arunachal Pradesh)
  • Loss of habitat = permanent global extinction
🌐

Alien Species

tap to explore ▼

ALIEN / EXOTIC

  • Species that are not originally from a particular habitat
  • Accidentally or deliberately introduced into a habitat
  • Examples: Lantana (plant), African catfish (Clarias gariepinus), Water hyacinth (Eichhornia)
  • Can displace native species and damage ecosystem balance
✅

Normal Species

tap to explore ▼

NORMAL

  • Species whose population levels are considered normal for their survival
  • Examples: Cattle, sal, pine, rodents
  • Do not require special conservation measures currently
  • Can become vulnerable if habitat destruction continues

05 · COMMUNITY & CONSERVATION

Community Conservation Movements

🤝 Conservation strategies are not new in India. Local communities have historically played a vital role in protecting forests and wildlife — often alongside, and sometimes independently of, government efforts.

🌳
Chipko Movement
Himalayas

Famous movement that not only successfully resisted deforestation in several areas but also showed that community afforestation with indigenous species can be enormously successful.

✊ Community-led · Anti-deforestation
⚖️
Sariska, Rajasthan
Sariska Tiger Reserve

Villagers fought against mining by citing the Wildlife Protection Act. A powerful example of communities using legal tools to protect wildlife habitats from industrial encroachment.

⚖️ Legal resistance · Mining opposed
🦌
Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri'
Alwar, Rajasthan

Inhabitants of five villages in Alwar district declared 1,200 hectares of forest as their own protected area. Created their own rules — no hunting, no outside encroachments, rejected government involvement.

🛡️ Self-governed · 1,200 ha protected
🌾
Beej Bachao Andolan
Tehri & Navdanya

Farmers and citizen groups showed that diversified crop production without synthetic chemicals is possible and economically viable. Reviving traditional conservation methods of ecological farming.

🌱 Seed saving · Organic farming
🕌
Sacred Groves
Tribal India

Nature worship — an age-old tribal belief. Preserved virgin forests called Sacred Groves (forests of God and Goddesses). Mundas & Santhal (Chota Nagpur) worship mahua & kadamba trees. Bishnoi villages protect blackbuck, nilgai and peacocks.

🙏 Traditional · Tribal conservation
🤝
Joint Forest Management
JFM · Since 1988

Programme involves local village institutions in management and restoration of degraded forest land. In return, communities get non-timber forest produce and a share in successfully protected timber. Odisha passed first JFM resolution in 1988.

🌳 Govt + Community · Win-win model

The clear lesson from the dynamics of both environmental destruction and reconstruction in India is that local communities everywhere have to be involved in some kind of natural resource management. Accept only those economic or developmental activities that are people-centric, environment-friendly and economically rewarding.

— NCERT · Contemporary India II, Chapter 2

📝 Short Answer Questions (30 words) NCERT Textbook

  1. What is biodiversity? Why is biodiversity important for human lives?
    Biodiversity (Biological Diversity) is the immense variety of wildlife and cultivated species, diverse in form and function, closely integrated through multiple networks of interdependencies. It is important because: (i) it preserves ecological diversity and life support systems — water, air and soil; (ii) it preserves the genetic diversity of plants and animals for better growth and breeding; (iii) agriculture depends on traditional crop varieties; (iv) fisheries depend on aquatic biodiversity; (v) millions of people depend on forests for their livelihood.
  2. How have human activities affected the depletion of flora and fauna? Explain.
    Human activities that have caused depletion of flora and fauna include: (i) Deforestation — clearing forests for agriculture, industry and urban expansion; (ii) Poaching — illegal hunting and trade in wildlife (e.g. tiger skins, ivory); (iii) Mining — destroys habitats and causes land degradation; (iv) Over-exploitation — unsustainable extraction of forest produce; (v) Agricultural modernisation — use of chemical pesticides and fertilisers harming soil biodiversity; (vi) Introduction of alien species — displacing native species; (vii) Environmental pollution — contaminating habitats. These have pushed many species towards extinction and caused rapid decline in forest cover.

📖 Long Answer Questions (120 words) NCERT Textbook

  1. Describe how communities have conserved and protected forests and wildlife in India.
    Communities across India have conserved forests and wildlife through various traditional and modern methods: (i) Sacred Groves — Tribal communities like the Mundas and Santhal of Chota Nagpur worship specific trees (mahua, kadamba). Bishnoi villages protect blackbuck, nilgai and peacocks. These beliefs have preserved virgin forests in pristine form. (ii) Chipko Movement — Communities in the Himalayas resisted deforestation and demonstrated successful community afforestation with indigenous species. (iii) Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri' — Five villages in Alwar, Rajasthan declared 1,200 hectares of forest as a self-protected community reserve with their own rules banning hunting and rejecting government involvement. (iv) Sariska villagers — Used the Wildlife Protection Act to fight against mining. (v) Beej Bachao Andolan (Tehri) and Navdanya — Promoted diversified crop production without synthetic chemicals, reviving traditional ecological farming. (vi) Joint Forest Management (JFM) — Since 1988, local village institutions manage degraded forest land under the Forest Department and receive non-timber forest produce and a share in timber in return. All these examples show that local communities are vital partners in conservation.
  2. Write a note on good practices towards conserving forest and wildlife.
    Good practices for conserving forests and wildlife include: (i) Legal Protection — Strict enforcement of the Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972, banning hunting and trade in wildlife; (ii) Protected Areas — Establishing national parks, wildlife sanctuaries and biosphere reserves to protect habitats and species; (iii) Project Tiger and similar projects — Targeted conservation for endangered species while preserving biotypes of sizeable magnitude; (iv) Community Participation — Involving local communities through JFM, recognising traditional conservation knowledge like Sacred Groves and respecting community-protected areas; (v) Ecological Farming — Promoting methods like those of Beej Bachao Andolan — diversified crop production without synthetic chemicals that maintain soil biodiversity; (vi) Education and Awareness — Making people sensitive to the environment to reduce insensitive exploitation; (vii) Afforestation — Community afforestation with indigenous species (as shown by Chipko Movement) to restore degraded forests; (viii) Accepting only people-centric, environment-friendly economic activities so development does not come at the cost of biodiversity.
